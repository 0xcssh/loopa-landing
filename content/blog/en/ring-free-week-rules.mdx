## What the ring-free week is actually for

After 3 weeks of continuous wear, you remove the ring and go 7 days without one. During that
week, hormone levels drop and most people get a withdrawal bleed, similar to a period on the
pill. It's a scheduling convention, not a medical requirement: the ring works because of the 3
weeks of continuous hormone delivery that came before it, not because of the week off.

That distinction matters because it's also what makes the week forgiving. A few days of delay
here doesn't undo three weeks of consistent protection the way a few days of inconsistent wear
would.

## The actual safe margin

- **7 days off: the standard schedule.** Insert your next ring on day 8.
- **Up to 10 days off total (3 extra days): still safe**, provided the ring you just removed was
  worn correctly for its full 3 weeks. No backup contraception needed.
- **Past 10 days off: treat it as a missed dose.** Insert a new ring right away and use condoms,
  or avoid sex, for the next 7 days.

## Skipping the break with continuous use

Continuous (or extended) use means inserting a new ring immediately after removing the old one,
with no ring-free week at all. It's a common way to reduce or eliminate withdrawal bleeding, and
many rings are approved for this use, but not all. If you've never done it before, it's worth a
quick check with whoever prescribed your ring, since dosing schedules differ slightly between
products.

## The mistake that actually causes failures

The risk in the ring-free week almost never comes from the break itself. It comes from losing
track of which day the break started, especially when the week overlaps with travel, a busy
stretch at work, or simply a schedule change. By the time day 12 or day 14 rolls around
unnoticed, the safe-extension window has already closed without anyone deciding to extend it on
purpose.
