## Same hormones, different failure mode

Both methods deliver a combination of estrogen and progestin (or progestin alone, for
progestin-only options) to prevent ovulation. On paper, with perfect use, they land in the same
range: over 99% effective. The number people actually experience, typical-use effectiveness, is
lower for both: around 91% for the pill, closer to 93% for the ring in most published estimates.

That gap between "perfect use" and "typical use" isn't a flaw in the hormones. It's a measure of
how often real people miss the exact timing the method requires.

## The action count is the real difference

- **The pill: about 30 timed actions per month.** Miss one by more than a few hours (for
  progestin-only pills) or a full day (for combination pills), and you're in a reduced-protection
  window that specific month.
- **The ring: 2 timed actions per month.** Insert once, remove once, roughly three to four weeks
  apart, with a multi-day buffer built into both.

Fewer required actions means fewer chances to get the timing wrong. That's the entire reason the
ring tends to close the gap between perfect-use and typical-use effectiveness better than the
pill does in practice, not because the hormones themselves work differently.

## Where the pill still wins

The ring isn't automatically the better choice for everyone. The pill offers more brand and
dosage variety, is easier to stop mid-cycle if a prescriber recommends it, and some people are
simply more comfortable with a daily pill than with vaginal insertion. Comfort and consistency
with the method you actually stick to matters more than the method that scores marginally higher
in a study.

## What actually predicts real-world success

Across both methods, the single biggest predictor of whether typical-use effectiveness holds up
is whether the user has a reliable system for the required timing, not which method they picked.
A pill user with a rock-solid daily alarm can outperform the published average. A ring user who
loses track of which week they're in can fall well below it.
